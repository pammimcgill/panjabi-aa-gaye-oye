# Asia — paired manuscript for community review

Research checked 11 October 2026. Source-backed review draft; not final historical or community approval. Fictional grandfather dialogue and imagined artwork are not eyewitness evidence. No Claude API research was executed.

Punjab is already in Asia. This route explores selected Punjabi Sikh histories elsewhere in Asia: Singapore, Hong Kong and connections with Malaya. It does not stand for every Punjabi community in Asia.

## 1. A Port and a New Chapter

Singapore · 1881

### Young Readers

“We are still in Asia, beta,” Grandpa says. “Punjab is in Asia too!”

Our story now takes us to Singapore. In March 1881, a group of Sikh men from Punjab arrived to work in the police. A history published by Singapore’s National Library Board records their arrival. [1]

Singapore was under British colonial rule. An outside power helped decide who was recruited and what work they did. [1]

Imagine a busy port, with many people going about their day. Our drawing is imagined. It does not show the exact arrival.

“A job is part of a person’s life,” Grandpa says. “It is never the whole person.”

### Grown-Ups

“Asia is not somewhere beyond Punjab,” Grandfather reminds us. “It is the larger continent Punjab belongs to.” This route follows selected connections within it.

The National Library Board’s police history records an assistant superintendent and fifty-four Sikhs arriving from Punjab on 26 March 1881. It describes subsequent recruitment and work in policing and guarding commercial sites. [1]

This is one documented strand of migration, not a claim that the first Punjabi person in Singapore arrived that day. Recruitment under colonial government also needs to be read as a relationship of power. A uniform should not become a shorthand for an entire community’s identity or for an inherited aptitude for an occupation.

The port in the comic is an imagined setting. It introduces movement and work without pretending to reproduce a photograph or a named recruit’s experience. To go further, we would need individual service records, family accounts and sources beyond the recruiting institution.

“Ask what the record can actually tell us,” Grandfather says. An arrival date establishes an event. It does not give us the traveler’s hopes, the family’s conversation, or every reason for taking the journey.

## 2. Another Harbor, Another History

Hong Kong · 1860s onwards

### Young Readers

“Turn to another harbor,” Grandpa says. “This is Hong Kong.”

Its police museum tells us that British authorities recruited Sikh men directly from Punjab from 1862. It also describes Sikh officers wearing turbans as part of their uniform. [1]

This is a different place and story from Singapore.

“A museum uniform gives us a clue,” Grandpa says. “But it cannot tell us everything about the person who wore it.”

Imagine looking at an old coat in a glass case. What questions would you ask? Who wore it? What did they do after work?

We should keep looking for family stories as well as official records.

### Grown-Ups

“One harbor does not explain another,” Grandfather says. We turn from Singapore to Hong Kong and move back to the 1860s.

The Hong Kong Police Museum dates direct Sikh recruitment from Punjab to 1862 and describes the accommodation of turbans within police dress. It also records the end of overseas recruitment of Indian and Pakistani officers in 1961. [1]

The museum tells this history from an institutional perspective. Some of its language evaluates groups collectively. We use its dated information without adopting stereotypes or treating an official account as a complete view of colonial society.

A uniform can show how an institution represented its workers. It cannot reveal all of a wearer’s relationships, beliefs or experiences outside service. The story would be stronger with individually attributed records and community testimony alongside the museum’s account.

Our harbor and museum scenes are illustrative, not specifications for a historically exact uniform. Singapore and Hong Kong were not one destination, and their recruitment dates should not be merged into a single migration event.

“Keep the place names beside the dates,” Grandfather suggests. “That small habit helps us respect the differences between lives.”

## 3. A Door Open to Travelers

Singapore and Malaya · 1924

### Young Readers

“Where could newcomers find a place to gather?” Grandpa asks.

In Singapore, the Silat Road gurdwara building was finished in 1924. A gurdwara is a Sikh place of worship. Its history says it was also meant to help house newcomers and people traveling onward. [1]

People in Singapore, Malaya and nearby countries helped pay for it. [1]

Imagine hands preparing a meal, sweeping a floor and making space for a guest.

Our scenes are imagined, but the work of caring for a shared place is something we can think about together.

“A welcome takes effort,” Grandpa says. “Whose helping hands might we forget to notice?”

### Grown-Ups

“A journey also needs places to pause,” Grandfather says.

The Central Sikh Gurdwara Board dates the completion of the Silat Road building to 1924. Its account says the institution was intended to meet religious needs and accommodate new arrivals and Sikhs traveling onward. Funding came from police personnel in Singapore and Malaya and from Sikh donors in neighboring countries. [1]

This places a local institution within wider networks of movement and support. It does not mean that every traveler stayed there, or that every donor shared the same circumstances. Malaya is the historical geographical term used in the source; it should not simply be replaced with the borders of present-day Malaysia.

A community building can be read through its everyday work as well as its opening date. Cooking, cleaning, arranging accommodation and maintaining rooms are useful questions for further oral-history research. We do not assign those tasks to named people without evidence.

The comic uses a symbolic gurdwara rather than an architectural reconstruction. “Look for the work behind a welcome,” Grandfather says. A doorway matters because of the people who keep it open, not only because a building stands there.

[1] [Central Sikh Gurdwara Board — Gurdwara Sahib Silat Road](https://sikhs.org.sg/about-srst/)

## 4. Care in a Time of War

Singapore and Malaya · 1942–1945

### Young Readers

“This page needs a gentle voice,” Grandpa says.

During the war in Singapore and Malaya, some families lost loved ones. The history of Silat Road gurdwara tells of widows and children receiving shelter and help. Widows and other Sikh civilians also helped keep the gurdwara working. [1]

They were people giving care as well as receiving it.

Our picture imagines a quiet meal and a place to rest. It does not show a battle or a named family.

“You can pause whenever you need,” Grandpa says. “History includes difficult days. It also asks us to notice the people who cared for others.”

### Grown-Ups

“When we speak of war, make room for the people keeping daily life going,” Grandfather says.

The Silat Road institutional history describes support for widows and children during the Japanese Occupation of 1942–1945. It also credits widows and other civilian Sikhs with sustaining the gurdwara’s functions. After the war, many widows returned to Punjab with free sea passage. [1]

The account brings care and civilian agency into a period often told through military events. It should not be stretched into a complete history of wartime Singapore or Malaya, or into an identical experience assigned to every Sikh family.

Those receiving assistance could also be organizers and contributors. That is why our narrative avoids depicting women and children only as passive figures waiting for rescue. At the same time, a brief institutional account cannot establish each person’s choices or the constraints they faced.

The comic is deliberately non-graphic. Its meal, bedding and supportive gestures are imagined, with no claim to reproduce an actual scene. “We can tell a difficult story without making up a survivor’s words,” Grandfather says. Readers may pause or choose to discuss it with someone they trust.

## 5. Women Make a Place to Meet

Singapore · 1946 onwards

### Young Readers

“Our last page opens another door,” Grandpa says.

After the Second World War, Sikh women in Singapore formed a group called Istri Sat Sang. They wanted a place to meet and take part in religious, social and cultural activities. The group’s history traces its beginning to 1946. [1]

Imagine friends planning a gathering together. Each person can bring a different idea.

These women help us see a story bigger than ships, police work or buildings.

“Aa gaye oye!” Grandpa smiles. “We have met people who made room for one another.”

What kind of gathering would you help create? Who would you invite to share an idea?

### Grown-Ups

“A place to belong can begin with people deciding to meet,” Grandfather says.

Istri Sat Sang Singapore’s own history traces its beginnings, through oral history, to 26 March 1946 at the Central Sikh Temple. It describes women seeking opportunities for religious, social and cultural participation. It distinguishes that beginning from the election of an executive council in 1963 and formal registration in 1964. [1]

These are different kinds of dates: a remembered founding, an organizational change and a legal registration. Keeping them separate is a small but important part of careful history. The institution’s account is also a starting point for further listening, not a substitute for every member’s experience.

The chapter broadens a route that began with male recruitment into colonial police forces. Women’s collective activity belongs within the central narrative of settlement and community life. It is not an optional addition after the supposed main story.

Our final gathering is imagined. “Aa gaye oye,” Grandfather says, “and there are still more voices to hear.” These five chapters cover selected Sikh histories beyond Punjab, not all of Asia’s Punjabi communities. That wider history remains an invitation to keep researching.
