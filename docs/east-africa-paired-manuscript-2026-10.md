# East Africa — paired manuscript for community review

Research checked 11 October 2026. Source-backed review draft; not final historical or community approval. Fictional grandfather dialogue and imagined artwork are not eyewitness evidence. No Claude API research was executed.

Follow selected histories in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda. These places have their own peoples and histories; Punjabi journeys are one part of a much larger story.

## 1. Across the Indian Ocean

1896–1901 · present-day Kenya

### Young Readers

“Find the Indian Ocean, beta,” Grandpa says. “Our next journey takes us toward East Africa.”

A railway called the Uganda Railway began at Mombasa in 1896. It reached Kisumu, beside Lake Victoria, in 1901. Both places are in Kenya today. [1]

People from India, many from Punjab, worked on its construction. We know this from an old report to the British Parliament. [2]

“This was not empty land waiting for newcomers,” Grandpa reminds us. “People already had homes and lives here.”

Our picture imagines a journey and a meeting at the coast. It cannot show every traveler’s route.

A railway’s name is only one clue. Always look closely at the map!

### Grown-Ups

“A name on a map can hide as much as it reveals,” Grandfather says.

Uganda Railways Corporation dates construction from Mombasa in 1896 to Kisumu, on Lake Victoria, in 1901. [1] The original line’s endpoints are in present-day Kenya; its name should not lead readers to place the entire construction story inside modern Uganda.

In May 1902, a British parliamentary answer described the large majority of the Indian construction workforce as Punjabi. [2] That is an official statement made in a colonial setting, not a passenger list or a complete account of the workers’ identities. We do not infer that every Indian worker was Sikh or that all came from the same district.

The wider landscape matters too. Migration into East Africa entered existing African societies; it did not begin their history. Following a railway should therefore raise questions about the communities whose land it crossed as well as the labor recruited to build it.

Our coastal arrival is illustrative. It gives no named person an undocumented itinerary. “First locate the places,” Grandfather suggests. “Then ask whose journey, and whose home, the map is showing.”

## 2. Tracks and Unequal Power

1902 · reading colonial records

### Young Readers

“Who built the tracks?” Grandpa asks. “And who made the rules?”

An old British Parliament debate mentions African and Indian railway workers. It describes different ways of paying them. The words in the old record are sometimes unfair and hurtful. [1]

We can learn from a record without agreeing with it.

The railway was built under British colonial rule. That means an outside power controlled the territory. Workers’ effort and the rulers’ power are both part of the story. [2]

Imagine looking at an old tool. It cannot tell us its owner’s name or how their day felt.

“Remember the people behind the work,” Grandpa says.

### Grown-Ups

“A railway is made of more than steel,” Grandfather says. “Look for the people and the power behind it.”

A December 1902 parliamentary answer discusses African and Indian labor on the Uganda Railway, describing different payment arrangements and making a racialized judgment about efficiency. [1] We cite the record to expose its framing, not to adopt that judgment. Its language reflects the unequal world in which the question was asked.

The railway’s institutional history places its construction within British East Africa. [2] The labor of Punjabi migrants therefore cannot be separated from colonial control, or celebrated as though no African communities were involved or affected.

Official records can help establish what authorities said and planned. They do not automatically reveal what workers thought, and they may minimize harms or leave people unnamed. A fuller account needs worker testimony and African historical perspectives alongside administrative documents.

Our comic uses an imagined worksite and a present-day discussion of evidence. It is not a measured reconstruction of a specific gang, uniform or task. “Give people credit for their work,” Grandfather says, “and keep asking who got to make the decisions.”

## 3. A Place to Gather

Dar es Salaam, Tanzania · a 1997 study

### Young Readers

“Let us visit a different place now,” Grandpa says. “Dar es Salaam is in Tanzania.”

A researcher named Richa Nagar studied community life there. Her work, published in 1997, describes Sikh families gathering at a gurdwara. People prayed, prepared food and shared important family occasions. [1]

A gurdwara is a Sikh place of worship. A community meal can bring people together.

“But each person has their own story,” Grandpa says. “Even people in the same room may experience life differently.”

Our drawing imagines people getting ready for a shared meal. It is not a photograph from the study.

What work helps a gathering feel welcoming?

### Grown-Ups

“We should not tell the whole region through one railway,” Grandfather says. This chapter turns to Dar es Salaam in Tanzania.

Richa Nagar’s 1997 study examines several Asian communities in the city. Its account of Sikh communal life describes the gurdwara as a setting for worship, preparing meals and marking family occasions. It also examines differences of gender and class within community spaces. [1]

This is evidence from a particular study and period, not a census of today’s community. Nor should its Sikh examples stand for every Punjabi or every Tanzanian Asian household. The author’s attention to unequal experiences helps us avoid imagining a community as a single voice.

A shared meal invites practical questions: who prepares it, who cleans afterwards, who can attend, and whose effort is easily overlooked? Those questions can make an apparently simple scene more revealing without inventing an answer for a real person.

The illustration is a composite gathering rather than a depiction of Nagar’s research participants. “Look beyond the building,” Grandfather suggests. “A place to gather is also made by the people who keep it going.”

## 4. When Home Was Taken Away

Uganda · 1972

### Young Readers

“This is a difficult page,” Grandpa says. “We can read it slowly.”

In 1972, Uganda’s ruler Idi Amin ordered Asian people to leave. Families were forced to leave places they called home. This is called expulsion. [1]

It was not a holiday or an ordinary move. People deserved safety and fair treatment.

“This happened in Uganda,” Grandpa says. “We must not tell it as though every country in East Africa did the same thing.”

Our picture shows an imagined family holding each other close. We do not know how every family felt.

You can pause here. When you are ready, we will learn how some journeys continued.

### Grown-Ups

“Not every departure is chosen,” Grandfather says. The story now moves to Uganda in 1972, not to East Africa as a single political unit.

The National Archives’ education pack traces Amin’s August expulsion orders, including their widening scope and the problems of nationality and statelessness they created. Its reproduced Cabinet record of 8 August refers to Asian British-passport holders being required to leave within three months. [1]

The changing orders and people’s different legal positions make a single uncomplicated label inadequate. We also should not equate all Ugandan Asians with Punjabis or Sikhs. The expulsion affected a broader population with varied family histories.

For children, the essential distinction is between choosing a move and being forced from home. For adult readers, the documents invite closer examination of state power, citizenship and the responsibilities of receiving governments. The record is not just about transport; it concerns people’s ability to remain, belong and live safely.

Our family scene is fictional and deliberately non-graphic. It attributes no belongings, words or emotions to a named survivor. Read at your own pace, and allow a family member to decline to discuss a painful memory.

## 5. Connections That Continue

1972 onwards · more than one destination

### Young Readers

“Where did people go next?” Grandpa asks.

They did not all go to the same country. A British Parliament record from December 1972 reports arrivals of Ugandan Asians in Canada, Australia and New Zealand. [1]

A number can show that people arrived. It cannot tell us each family’s whole story.

Imagine a letter crossing another ocean. Someone opens it, and a familiar name brings faraway people a little closer.

That letter is part of our imagined picture, not a real family’s record.

“Aa gaye oye,” Grandpa says softly. “A new chapter can begin without erasing the old one.”

What would you like a new friend to know about your story?

### Grown-Ups

“An arrival does not erase the place someone left,” Grandfather says.

A parliamentary answer dated 11 December 1972 reported that, by 1 December, Ugandan Asians had arrived in Canada, Australia and New Zealand. It distinguished arrivals from visa applications approved or still under consideration. [1] This is a dated snapshot, not a final total of resettlement and not evidence that all families followed one route.

The distinction is a useful reading habit: a planned journey, a permission to travel and an actual arrival are different events. Numbers can establish scale or movement, but they cannot supply a family’s unrecorded feelings or tell us whether relatives were reunited.

Our East Africa route has visited construction in present-day Kenya, communal life in Tanzania and forced departure from Uganda. These selected episodes do not form a single family biography or a complete regional history. Connections between them must be demonstrated, not assumed.

The illustrated letters and reunion are imagined. If a relative wishes to share a real memory, note which places and dates they identify and preserve any uncertainty. “There can be more than one home in a story,” Grandfather says. “Listen carefully to the names people give them.”
