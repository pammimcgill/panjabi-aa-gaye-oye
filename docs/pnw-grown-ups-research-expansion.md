# Pacific Northwest — Grown-Ups research expansion (editorial draft)
**9 October 2026 · Research method:** independent institutional web sources. **Not Claude-generated.** Not yet community-reviewed. The existing adult manuscript remains the editorial reference; this is an evidence-backed expansion for integration after line-by-line comparison.

## Chapter 1 — A journey begins in Punjab
Imagine an early-twentieth-century Punjabi household considering a departure across the world. The decision was rarely just about one traveler. A journey could separate spouses, parents and children, and those who stayed home continued to shape the migrant's life through letters, money and family expectations. We should not invent one typical family, nor suggest all Punjabis had the same religion, class, or reasons for leaving.

In the early 1900s, Punjabi migrants began forming communities on Canada's Pacific coast. Their routes were shaped by the British Empire: India and Canada belonged to the same imperial system, yet racial restrictions denied Indian subjects the freedom of movement many expected from that shared status. The contrast between imperial citizenship and unequal immigration rules became stark in the 1914 Komagata Maru incident [1].

A tangible early trace appears in Golden, British Columbia. The Golden Museum and Archives identifies a **20 July 1902 telegram** naming Geha Singh of Golden, a document placing South Asian residents in that town at the beginning of the century [2]. The record gives us a name and date; it does not tell us Geha Singh's thoughts or family story. Those remain unknown.

## Chapter 2 — Ports, ships and the Pacific
Travel from Punjab to the Pacific Northwest was not a single route marked on a modern map. Migrants could move through multiple Asian ports, and the voyage of one ship should never stand in for everyone else's journey.

The **Komagata Maru** provides an unusually well-documented example. Parks Canada records that the ship reached Vancouver on **23 May 1914** after traveling via Hong Kong and Japan. Its **376 passengers** included **340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims and 12 Hindus** [1]. Those numbers are a reminder that the broad category “Indian” hid religious and social differences.

For travelers, the destination could be visible long before entry was possible. The Komagata Maru remained in Vancouver waters for two months while officials prevented most passengers from landing. The people aboard were not merely passive victims: passengers and supporters challenged the restrictions in court, and local South Asian residents raised funds and organized legal help [1].

## Chapter 3 — Work, worship and the beginnings of community
Work made migration possible, but the kinds of jobs available were not neutral. Punjabi newcomers worked in lumber mills and related industries on the Pacific coast. The Golden Museum documents employment at the **Columbia River Lumber Company** in its lumberyard, planer and sawmill [2]. The story should show both the physical labor and the ways people created community beyond their workplaces.

One particularly significant landmark is Vancouver's **Khalsa Diwan Society gurdwara at 1866 West 2nd Avenue**, constructed in **1908**. The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes its religious, social and political importance to early South Asian communities in British Columbia [3]. A gurdwara is a Sikh place of worship, but early community institutions could also provide social connection and practical help.

The Pacific coast story extends beyond Canada and Washington. University of California, Davis's Punjabi and Sikh Diaspora Digital Archive identifies the **Stockton Gurdwara**, founded in **1912**, as the first Sikh house of worship established in the United States. It documents a community space serving Sikhs, Muslims, Hindus and Catholic wives and American-born children of Punjabi pioneers [4]. This is a documented California connection, not evidence that every migrant traveled through Stockton.

## Chapter 4 — Exclusion, violence and resistance
The history of arrival must also include the history of people trying to prevent arrival. In **Bellingham, Washington, in September 1907**, white mobs attacked South Asian workers and forced people from their homes. A local Washington gurdwara's historical account describes the attack and its context in anti-Asian organizing [5]. Further review should prioritize city records, contemporary reporting and university archives before adding casualty figures or dramatic scenes.

In Vancouver in 1914, the Komagata Maru passengers encountered exclusion enforced through Canada's immigration system. Parks Canada identifies the **continuous journey regulation** among the restrictions used to keep them out. The standoff lasted two months; supporters mounted a legal challenge, but most passengers were forced to leave. When the ship returned to India, colonial authorities used deadly force at Budge Budge. Parks Canada reports **20 passengers died** in that confrontation [1].

This is not a tale in which unfairness simply disappears. The historical record also preserves solidarity, organizing and resistance. The story should center the people affected and avoid making racism into a puzzle children must solve to unlock the next page.

## Chapter 5 — Aa gaye oye: making home across borders
Arrival was not one triumphant moment at a dock. It unfolded over years: through work, friendships, institutions, families and struggles over belonging. Vancouver's 1908 gurdwara and Stockton's 1912 gurdwara anchor this history in specific, documented places [3][4].

The Stockton archive also reveals a richer picture than a story solely about men in mills: religious communities interacted, families included women from different backgrounds, and American-born children grew up with multiple inheritances [4]. This history deserves additional dedicated research on Punjabi women, family formation, Indigenous communities and later generations; none should be invented to fill archival silence.

The closing phrase **“Aa gaye oye!” — “We've arrived!”** is our editorial motif, not a historical quotation. It invites readers to ask a more interesting question: what does it mean to belong to a place while remembering another?
