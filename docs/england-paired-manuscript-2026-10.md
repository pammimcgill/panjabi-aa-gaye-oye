# England — paired manuscript for community review

Research checked 11 October 2026. Source-backed review draft; not final historical or community approval. Fictional grandfather dialogue and imagined artwork are not eyewitness evidence. No Claude API research was executed.

From an early royal exile to Southall’s working families: five windows into Punjabi lives in England. These are selected histories, not one route followed by everyone.

## 1. An Earlier Journey

1849–1854

### Young Readers

“Our story has more than one beginning, beta,” Grandpa says.

Long before the Southall families in our next chapter, a young ruler from Punjab came to England. His name was Duleep Singh. British rulers had taken over Punjab in 1849. He lost his kingdom and was brought to London in 1854. [1]

“That was not a holiday,” Grandpa says gently. “Power can change where people are able to live.”

A museum keeps portraits of him. A picture can help us ask questions. But it cannot tell us every thought he had.

What would you ask about the person in a portrait?

### Grown-Ups

“Let us open an earlier page,” our fictional grandfather says. Punjabi connections with England did not begin with migration after the Second World War.

The Royal Collection Trust records that the British annexed Punjab in 1849 and compelled Duleep Singh to give up his sovereignty. He was brought to London in 1854. The Trust also preserves portraits and photographs of him. [1]

This was a journey shaped by conquest and unequal power. It should not be used as a cheerful travel adventure, or as a typical account of later workers’ migration. A royal exile and a family seeking employment belong to different circumstances.

The portraits invite another question: who had the resources to commission an image, and whose life was left unpictured? A museum portrait is evidence of how someone was represented. It is not permission to invent private conversations or emotions.

Grandfather pauses. “We can be curious without filling every silence ourselves.” Keep that thought as the story moves forward by a century, from an unusually well-documented individual to the memories of working families.

## 2. A Suitcase in Southall

1950s–1960s

### Young Readers

“Now turn forward many years,” Grandpa says. “Let us visit Southall, in England.”

An oral-history project tells of Punjabi people who moved there in the 1950s and 1960s. They looked for work and places to live. They built businesses and community life too. [1]

Imagine arriving on an unfamiliar street. Who could help you find your way? Our illustrated family is made up, but the history of these journeys is real.

“Not every family came together,” Grandpa reminds us. “And one family’s story cannot stand for everyone.”

A suitcase holds things. A story can hold the people and places we remember.

### Grown-Ups

“A suitcase tells us someone travelled,” Grandfather says. “It does not tell us the whole reason why.”

The community oral-history project Panjabis of Southall describes Punjabi migration in the 1950s and 1960s, including the difficulties of securing work and housing and establishing businesses and cultural life. [1]

These connected tasks are a useful starting point for this chapter. Settlement was an ongoing process, not an instant transformation at a station or airport. Yet we should resist giving all newcomers one motive, occupation or timetable. Southall is a particular place, not a substitute for all of England.

Our illustrated arrival is an imagined composite. No named participant is assigned its suitcase, letter or dialogue. The project’s published description supports the themes here; this edition does not claim to have transcribed or checked every interview in its film.

If your family has a migration story, ask what happened before arrival as well as afterwards. Let the storyteller decide what to share. A careful question can open a door without assuming that every memory is easy.

## 3. The Women Who Tell the Story

After Partition · later twentieth century

### Young Readers

“Whose stories have we not heard yet?” Grandpa asks.

A project called Foreign Land records women’s memories. Many of the women were Punjabi. They went to England looking for work in the years after India was divided in 1947. Their stories lead us to Southall. [1]

Women belong at the center of this history. We should ask them about their work, choices and friendships.

Imagine sitting with an older relative and listening. You might ask, “What would you like children to know?”

“Then give them time,” Grandpa says. “Listening is part of learning.”

### Grown-Ups

“Before we close the album, ask who has been left outside its pages,” Grandfather says.

Sheba Remy Kharbanda’s Vilayati Tarti / Foreign Land project centers women, mostly Punjabi, who moved to England for work in the decades after Partition and made lives in Southall. Its creator describes an archive of eighteen stand-alone interviews alongside a short film, inspired by her maternal grandmother. [1]

This changes the questions we bring to migration history. We should not automatically describe women only as people waiting at home or joining someone else. Paid work, household responsibilities, aspirations and relationships all deserve investigation; their balance must be established for each life rather than assumed.

The summary here draws on the creator’s project description, not a completed review of the interview recordings. The imagined workplace in the comic is not evidence that any named interviewee held a particular job.

A useful family-history exercise is to ask the same open question of different generations. Record the speaker’s own words only with permission. If memories differ, preserve the difference and look for supporting records instead of forcing everyone into a single tidy account.

## 4. Standing Together

1956 onwards

### Young Readers

“Finding a home should not mean accepting unfair treatment,” Grandpa says.

In Southall, the Indian Workers’ Association helped workers and challenged discrimination. Discrimination means treating people unfairly because of who they are. The association also offered advice and support. [1]

Imagine a meeting where neighbors take turns speaking. One person has a problem. Others listen and think about how to help.

Our meeting is imagined. The organization was real.

“People do not have to solve every problem alone,” Grandpa says. “A fair community makes room for each voice.”

What helps everyone get a turn when your group makes a decision?

### Grown-Ups

“Who helps when a problem is bigger than one household?” Grandfather asks.

The Indian Workers’ Association oral-history project follows the organization’s Southall work and campaigns from 1956 to 2016. It describes opposition to discrimination, support for workers and a welfare and advice service. Its interviews include people active in the association and their families. [1]

Collective organization belongs in a migration story alongside employment and family life. Newcomers were not simply recipients of welcome or hostility: they organized, argued and created institutions. That observation should not flatten differences among members or suggest one organization represented every Punjabi person.

There is also an earlier thread of public action. London Museum documents Sophia Duleep Singh’s campaigning for women’s voting rights and tax resistance in the early twentieth century. [2] Her campaign and the IWA’s work were distinct movements, separated by time and purpose. Placing them beside each other invites comparison, not a claim of direct organizational continuity.

The comic’s meeting and march are symbolic scenes. Before reconstructing a particular protest, we would need its date, participants and documentary evidence. For now, our question is simple: how can people act together to challenge exclusion?

## 5. Aa Gaye Oye — Keep the Story

Remembering across generations

### Young Readers

“Aa gaye oye! We have arrived at another page,” Grandpa smiles.

Southall’s history includes people building places to gather and standing up against unfairness. Community projects have recorded some of their stories. [1]

“But a story is not finished because we have written it down,” he adds. “There are more people to listen to.”

Imagine opening an album together. Ask who is in each picture. Ask where it was taken. It is fine if nobody knows yet!

You can feel at home in England and care about Punjab too. You do not have to choose just one memory to keep.

What story would you pass to the next reader?

### Grown-Ups

“Home is not the last stop on a map,” Grandfather says. “It is a relationship people keep making.”

Panjabis of Southall places community institutions and campaigns against racial inequality within its account of settlement. [1] These themes remind us that belonging can involve both everyday care and public action. They do not establish that everyone experienced Southall in the same way.

The five chapters are selected windows: an earlier exile, postwar settlement, women’s memories, collective action and the work of remembering. They are not a complete history of Punjabis in England. Sikh histories are part of that wider story; Punjabi identity should not be treated as the name of only one religion.

To continue at home, choose an object and ask its owner to describe it. Note a date only when it is known. Separate a remembered detail from something independently documented, and do not publish a recording or private photograph without permission.

“Aa gaye oye,” Grandfather says again. The phrase closes our reading journey, not the history itself. The next generation may ask questions we have not thought of. Leaving room for those questions is one way of keeping a story alive.
