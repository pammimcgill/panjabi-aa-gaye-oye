How Punjabi men found work in early British Columbia

Step back to 1902 and watch how the first Punjabi men built new lives in the mills, farms and rail lines of British Columbia. This is a sourced story about hard work, unfair treatment, and the start of a community.

1

Arriving in Golden

In 1902, the first Sikh men arrived in Golden, British Columbia, to work at a sawmill run by the Columbia River Lumber Company. This small settlement was an early sign of what would grow across the province.

2

Counting the Newcomers

In 1904, Canadian officials opened an immigration office to record how many people arrived from India. That year only 45 people came, but thousands more would follow within a few years.

3

Soldiers Who Stayed

Many of the Punjabi men arriving were former soldiers who had worked the land back home. Some had seen British Columbia before while serving in British Indian Army regiments, and they said its rivers and mountains reminded them of Panjab.

4

Working in the Mills

Between 1904 and 1908, about 5,000 Punjabi men arrived in British Columbia. Many found work in sawmills along the Fraser River and False Creek, in places like Kitsilano, Fraser Mills and Queensborough, including at the Seymour Lumber Company.

5

Harder Jobs, Lower Pay

At mills like Seymour Lumber Company, Punjabi workers were given the hardest jobs, called 'unskilled,' and paid less than white workers. They were also the first to lose their jobs when work slowed down.

6

Farms, Rails and Worship

Beyond the mills, Punjabi men worked on farms, in construction, and laying railway track across the Fraser Valley. By 1909, dozens worked on farms in Mission and Matsqui, and many more worked in mills in Abbotsford and Harrison Mills. Workers also began gathering to worship together in rented rooms and homes, long before any temple was built.
