1908: the continuous-journey rule and the $200 requirement

How Canada's 1908 rules made Punjabi family reunion almost impossible

Step back to 1908, when new Canadian rules made it nearly impossible for Punjabi families to reunite. Discover how one community faced these barriers and refused to give up.

1

Crossing Oceans for Work

In the early 1900s, many Punjabi migrants, including many Sikh men, settled in British Columbia. They worked in mills, on farms, and on railways, believing that as British subjects they could travel freely within the empire.

2

A Violent Welcome

In September 1907, South Asian mill workers were violently driven out of Bellingham, Washington, and a riot against Asian communities broke out in Vancouver days later. These events were part of a wider climate of exclusion on both sides of the border.

3

The Continuous-Journey Rule

In 1908, Canada introduced a rule requiring immigrants to travel non-stop from their home country on a through ticket bought there. Although a steamship company had offered direct routes from India, the government blocked the sale of those tickets, making the journey nearly impossible.

4

The $200 Barrier

That same year, Canada raised the amount of money immigrants from India needed to show at landing from $50 to $200. For many laborers this was an enormous sum, making it even harder for people to join family already in Canada.

5

The Komagata Maru

These rules were challenged, most famously in 1914 when the ship Komagata Maru arrived in Vancouver carrying 376 passengers, most of whom were refused permission to land. Between 1910 and 1920, only 112 Indian immigrants were allowed into Canada.

6

Families Reunited at Last

After years of pressure from Sikh communities in British Columbia, a 1919 order finally allowed some wives and children to join family members already living in Canada. Despite the hardships, these communities endured, organized, and kept challenging unfair laws.
