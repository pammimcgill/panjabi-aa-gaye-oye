September 1907: the anti-Asian riot in Vancouver

How anger and fear turned into violence in 1907

Come look back at a hard week in 1907, when fear and unfair blame turned into real harm for Asian communities. A curious child guide will help you see what happened, and what the old records still argue about.

1

Two Cities, One Storm

In the first days of September 1907, anger against Asian immigrants boiled over in two Pacific Northwest cities. Many Punjabi workers, most of them Sikh, had come to Bellingham to work in the lumber mills. The trouble in both cities grew from campaigns that tried to push Asian neighbors out.

2

A Mob in Bellingham

On September 4, white labor activists accused Punjabi workers of taking jobs and pushed mill owners to fire them. That night, a mob broke into the workers' lodging houses, wrecked their belongings, and chased men toward the edge of town or the police station.

3

Jail for Safety, Then Flight

Some Punjabi workers spent the night locked in the city jail, which was meant to keep them safe. Within just a few days, almost every South Asian worker had left Bellingham. Old records disagree on exact numbers—one historian counts over 200 workers forced out, while another source says about 500 people joined the mob.

4

Rally Turns to Riot

Three days later, on September 7, a group called the Asiatic Exclusion League held a rally in Vancouver. Thousands of people marched together, including local leaders. Part of the crowd broke away and attacked Chinatown, smashing windows and damaging shops.

5

Japantown Stands Together

The violence spread into Japantown, where residents organized to defend their neighborhood. The unrest did not stop right away—it continued into the days that followed. Chinese and Japanese residents, not Punjabi workers, were the main targets of the Vancouver attacks.

6

What the Records Show

Historians still disagree about crowd sizes, injuries, and arrests from that week, so this story sticks to what is well documented. Afterward, officials looked into paying back Chinese and Japanese residents for their losses, and new rules in 1908 made it even harder for people to migrate from India to Canada.
