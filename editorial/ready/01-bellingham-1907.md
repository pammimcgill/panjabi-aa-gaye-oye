### Summary

In the first days of September 1907, a crowd of white workers in Bellingham, Washington, attacked the bunkhouses of Punjabi millworkers and forced them out of town. The episode took place a short distance south of the Canadian border and is one of the earliest chapters of South Asian history in the Pacific Northwest.

### Article

Bellingham in 1907 was a lumber town. Its mills drew labor from across the Pacific coast, and by that year they also employed several hundred South Asian men, most of them Punjabis, and most of them Sikhs. Estimates of their number vary by source, from roughly 200 to about 250 workers in the town itself. Many had crossed the border from British Columbia, where far larger numbers had arrived that same year.

Newspapers of the day called the men "Hindus," which was wrong on both counts: most were Sikhs from Panjab, and the label turned a religion into a slur. Newspapers portrayed them as a "dusky peril." Behind the language was an economic fear. The men worked for lower wages, lived frugally in crowded bunkhouses and sent much of their pay home to families in India.

## The days of the riot

Local agitation was led by an exclusion league chapter of about 800 members. Sources name it as the Japanese-Korean Exclusion League or the Asiatic Exclusion League. On Labor Day, September 2, more than 1,000 union members paraded through town and demanded that no South Asians be employed in the mills after that day. The men went to work anyway.

On the evening of September 4 a mob, which accounts put at anywhere from 400 to 600 people, went to the mills and the bunkhouses. Men were pulled from their work and their beds. Property was destroyed or taken. Some men were beaten, and many were driven toward the city limits. More than a hundred spent the night in the city jail in the basement of City Hall, under an arrangement with the police chief: they were held for their "protection," on the condition that they leave.

They did. Within days nearly all of the South Asian workers had left, by train or steamship for points south, or on foot back to Canada. The last few were evicted by September 17. Sources disagree about the injuries, and some list several men taken to hospital. No one was killed. A handful of arrests were made, and no one from the mob was punished.

## Afterward

Many of the men returned to British Columbia and others made their way to Oregon and California, where mills and farms were hiring. For decades afterward, most South Asians avoided Bellingham and Whatcom County. Historians place the riot within a wider pattern: Bellingham had expelled Chinese residents in 1885 and would see Japanese Americans removed in 1942, and contemporary accounts link the 1907 violence to anti-Asian unrest along the Pacific coast that year, including in British Columbia.

The event has since become a subject of remembrance. Documentary films, including *Present in All That We Do* (2007) and *We're Not Strangers* (2013), have retold it, and the Arch of Healing and Reconciliation project is part of the remembrance.

## In the story of arrival

1907 was a peak year for early arrivals. According to one reference source, more than two thousand South Asian men, most of them Sikhs, reached Canada that year, and over a thousand entered the United States, some of them crossing the border from Canada. Bellingham shows the other side of that arrival: the first organized effort to make sure the newcomers could not stay.

## Why it matters here

For Punjabi families in Seattle and Vancouver, the story is close to home. It shows that the community's earliest years in this region were shaped by exclusion as well as work, and that settlement here was contested from the start. It also shows how a name given to a community by others, "Hindu," can obscure who its people were.
