### Summary

In May 1914 a Japanese steamship carrying 376 passengers from Panjab, nearly all of them Sikhs, anchored in Burrard Inlet and was refused entry to Canada. The passengers were British subjects. Their two months in Vancouver harbour became one of the best-known episodes in the history of Canadian immigration law.

### Article

By 1914 Canada had made it almost impossible for people from India to immigrate. A rule known as the continuous-journey regulation required immigrants to arrive by an uninterrupted voyage from their country of origin, a journey that was practically impossible to make from India. Immigrants from Asia were also expected to hold $200 in cash. The rules did not name India, but they were widely seen as a way to keep Indian arrivals out.

Gurdit Singh, a Sikh businessman, set out to test them. He chartered a Japanese steamship, the Komagata Maru, and sold tickets for a direct voyage. The ship left Hong Kong in April with a first group of passengers, picked up more in Shanghai and Yokohama, and sailed from Yokohama in early May with 376 people on board: 340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims and 12 Hindus, all from Panjab. They saw themselves as British subjects with a right to travel within the Empire.

## Two months at anchor

The ship reached Burrard Inlet on May 23, 1914, and was not allowed to dock. Immigration officials let only a small number of returning residents come ashore. Sources give the figure as 20 or 22. The rest stayed on board without adequate provisions while the ship remained anchored until July 23.

Ashore, Vancouver's South Asian community organized. A committee of local men hired the lawyer Edward Bird to represent the passengers, and members of the Khalsa Diwan Society helped raise funds and supplies. A court challenge failed when the British Columbia Court of Appeal upheld the federal government's decision. The mood in official circles was open about the aim. According to a Canadian Sikh Heritage account, British Columbia's premier said the day before the ship arrived that the province had to remain a "white man's country."

On July 23 the Komagata Maru was turned away and sailed for Asia.

## Budge Budge

The ship reached Budge Budge, near Calcutta, in late September 1914. British Indian authorities, already anxious about the Ghadar movement and suspecting revolutionary ties among the passengers, tried to arrest Gurdit Singh and others. A clash followed. Accounts say 19 or 20 passengers were killed, and many more were jailed or forced into hiding. Gurdit Singh escaped and lived underground until he gave himself up in 1922.

## In the story of arrival

The Komagata Maru is the story of an arrival that was refused. The passengers had come the way the law seemed to allow, as British subjects on a chartered ship, and were turned back. It is also part of why the local community organized: the committee, the lawyer and the funds that appeared on the dock were the early institutions of a community that had decided to stay.

## Remembering

For decades the story was told mainly inside the community. In 2008 Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologized at an event with Sikh Canadians in Surrey, but many wanted a formal apology in Parliament. On May 18, 2016, Prime Minister Justin Trudeau gave it in the House of Commons, apologizing for the laws that had allowed Canada to be indifferent to the passengers' plight. He noted that Canada could not be blamed alone for every tragedy that followed. The apology was announced at Vaisakhi on Parliament Hill.

The Komagata Maru remains a reference point in discussions of who belongs and who is allowed to arrive.
