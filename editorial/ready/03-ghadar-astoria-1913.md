### Summary

In 1913, Punjabi millworkers and student organizers met in Astoria, Oregon, and formed the group that became the Ghadar Party, a movement aimed at ending British rule in India. Its beginnings on the Pacific coast connect Oregon, California and British Columbia to the history of Panjab.

### Article

Early in the twentieth century, roughly five to six hundred South Asian men lived along the Columbia River in Oregon, from The Dalles to the coast, with the largest numbers in St. Johns and Astoria. Newspapers called them "Hindus," a misnomer, since most were Sikhs from Panjab, joined by Hindus and Muslims from elsewhere in India. They had left because of famine, plague and political conflict under British rule, and they worked mainly in lumber mills from British Columbia to California.

The work was hard, and the mills were also where organizing took root. According to historian Johanna Ogden's recent study, as summarized by AsAmNews, laborers and intellectuals, predominantly Sikhs from Panjab, built what she calls a revolutionary corridor from Astoria to The Dalles, and local newspapers noted their presence often.

## Astoria

In the spring of 1913, the Oregon Encyclopedia records, men of the community formed the radical nationalist organization in Astoria. Other accounts describe a notice in the Astoria Budget in May calling a meeting, and give July 15, 1913, as the date of its formal founding. Sources differ, so the date is best given as spring and summer of 1913. The group was first called the Pacific Coast Hindustan Association. Its president was Sohan Singh Bhakna, a mill worker, and its general secretary was Lala Har Dayal, an intellectual who had arrived from Europe.

Ghadar means revolution in Hindi and Urdu. Unlike the Indian National Congress, which favored non-violence, the party argued for armed revolution.

## A press and a paper

Funds raised from laborers and from Indian students at the University of California, Berkeley, paid for a headquarters and printing press in San Francisco, known as the Yugantar Ashram. Its weekly paper, Hindustan Ghadar, first appeared in Urdu on November 1, 1913, and in Punjabi on December 9. The paper aimed to reach Indian soldiers in the British army.

Its early membership was mostly Punjabi, drawn from Punjabis living on the West Coast of the United States and Canada. Its ties to the Komagata Maru voyage were also close: Gurdit Singh publicly expressed support for the Ghadar cause in Hong Kong in early 1914, and British intelligence suspected Ghadarites among the passengers.

## Return and repression

Within a year of the Astoria meeting, hundreds of Ghadarites, mostly laborers from the West Coast, went back to India hoping to spark an uprising. Most were captured, detained, tried or executed. Conspiracy trials followed in Lahore and in San Francisco. Historians note that the party's secular politics brought people of different castes and religions together in a way that left a lasting mark on Indian nationalism. In Panjab its members are remembered each year at the Gadri Mela.

## In the story of arrival

Ghadar shows arrival turning into organizing. Within a few years of reaching the coast, mill workers who had come to earn money were building a political movement with its own newspaper. The men who had been called "Hindus" and turned away or driven out of mill towns were also writing, printing and raising funds in three languages.

## In the Pacific Northwest

The Ghadar story is usually told from Oregon and California, but the men who built it lived and worked in the same coastal mill towns as the Punjabi communities of Bellingham and Vancouver.
